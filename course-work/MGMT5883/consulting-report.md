# Creativity under Trial Overload in a Multinational Clinical Research Organization

*A consulting report  |  October 2026*

## Introduction

This report concerns a multinational clinical-research organization where I worked for six and a half years, first as a Clinical Research Associate and later as an Associate Clinical Project Manager. The company runs clinical trials and delivers results to internal functions and to external clients. Creativity here means a novel and useful improvement in how a trial is run. Innovation means that improvement is carried out. I identify the obstacles that kept staff from that kind of creativity, then recommend one feasible action for each of the two most serious obstacles, and the steps I would use to put those actions in place.

## Obstacles to creativity and innovation

Turnover makes both worse. Vacancies are hard to fill, and joiners are mostly new graduates or people seeking faster promotion. Two obstacles do the direct damage.

The first is concurrent overload. Associates in peer companies handle about five trials, a figure I take from former colleagues in those firms. Here the same role carried eight to ten at once. Quieter trials still need routine work. When several are active, client requests and dated tasks fill the day. A view of the whole study appears only in a quiet stretch. Hospital documents for a human trial are the clear case: they must be collected and uploaded to the company database, they are less urgent than a client deadline, and they are omitted when the week is full. Leave does not create real cover. Client and hospital mail accumulates. A backup associate can take some urgent items, but study access sits with the responsible associate, so the backup can do only the easy tasks. Amabile (1998) treats time as a resource that must reach a threshold of sufficiency; below it, effort goes into coping. Extreme pressure also cuts creative thinking on that day and the days after (Amabile et al., 2002, as taught in Session 4). The omitted hospital file is the whole-study picture that pressure never allows.

The second obstacle is that help has no place in the priority order. The hierarchy gives each person a small piece of the trial. Colleagues rarely step in, because their own list is full and the task often sits outside their access. During internal database audits, associates ask assistants to update the records. Those requests lose to external client work: they are less urgent, and they are not billable. Assistants have no buffer. Management then cannot see the current status of the study. Amabile (1998) includes help through difficulty, and information that actually moves, among the conditions for creativity. IDEO's helping study adds that help fails when the right person is overloaded, so slack has to be designed (Amabile, Fisher, and Pillemer, 2014). If that status fact stays in one queue, managers work from a partial picture (Thompson, chapter 9). Staff also rarely see past their own fragment, and new graduates inherit it.

## Recommendations and how I would implement them

For the overload, I recommend a staffed cap of about five active trials per associate. The cap needs people. I would ask the team for the missing headcount: count the active trials, divide by five, and subtract the associates already in the role. Pay has to rise with those seats. The present load is known in the industry, and the people who accept it are mostly new graduates or candidates seeking a fast promotion. A higher salary is how associates who have already run a whole trial will take the job. That is Amabile's expertise component. It is the resource that lets the load reach a threshold of sufficiency. Pay above a fair load does not buy creativity. Pay that brings the load to about five, and brings people who can see a whole study, is the money the 1998 article says is missing (Amabile, 1998).

Every study would have at least two associates with access. The second person's authority is limited to a written list of items that cannot wait, so cover is standing and a week of leave does not freeze the study. IDEO's helping article is direct: a person who cannot reach the work cannot help (Amabile, Fisher, and Pillemer, 2014). I would make one request. In the first month I would count active trials and write the headcount and pay case. In the second month I would hire against that number and pair every study. In the third month, once the active load is near five, the quieter trials would get a weekly block for the hospital documents that are now omitted. The test is the load itself. Active trials sit near five per person, and the document queue is reviewed every week. Where one deadline is genuinely urgent, Session 4 still applies: protect that person from every other interruption and state why the job is vital (Amabile et al., 2002).

For the failure of help, I recommend taking routine administration off the associates, so they have slack for work that is not on a client deadline. University interns would do defined administration: collecting and filing hospital documents, and preparing the papers in a payment to a hospital. An AI system would draft routine reports, categorize documents, and prepare the hospital payment steps. Associates keep the judgment: whether the file is complete, whether the report is accurate, and whether the payment is right. Session 4 teaches that a model can raise the output of a piece of work and can also make the drafts more alike, while the decision that something is useful stays with the person who knows the study (Doshi and Hauser, 2024, as taught). That freed time is the slack IDEO says helping requires (Amabile, Fisher, and Pillemer, 2014). The database update can then sit in the week, owned by a named person, because the hour is no longer lost to filing.

The intern's list would be written, and it would stop short of client decisions. Amabile's challenge rule is to match the task to the person: an intern learns the administration, and the associate is no longer the only one who can touch a document (Amabile, 1998). The AI tools would start on three streams only: report drafts, document categories, and hospital payments. The associate clinical project manager would still read the date of the last database update, so a stale status is visible before an audit. Thompson's chapter is why that line exists: the fact has to leave one person's queue. Interns and the tools can start in the same quarter as the headcount request. They ease the present team while the new associates are being found.

## Conclusion

Creativity in this organization is a useful improvement in how a trial is run: a complete document set, a status a manager can trust, and a second associate who can cover the items that cannot wait. The two actions are a staffed cap near five, with pay that can attract experienced people and two-person access on every study, and a shift of routine administration to interns and to AI so that regular staff have time to see the whole trial. Headcount, pay, access, interns, and tools are one design. Each piece fails if the others are left out.
