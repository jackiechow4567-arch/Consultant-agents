## Nest Fresh Eggs (A)

**Case:** HBS 806-056, Kathleen L. McGinn and Alison Comings, copyright 2005. Decision window: August 2005. Protagonist: Cyd (Cynthia Osborne) Szymanski, CEO, "the Egglady."

### Stakeholder map

| Who | Stake in the decision |
|-----|------------------------|
| Cyd Szymanski | Owner-operator, brand, integrity, mother of AnnaBelle (born 2000), long-term intent to sell eventually, not now |
| AnnaBelle, Steve Szymanski (second husband, advisor, not in the business), nanny Danni | Personal success definition she states after becoming a parent: good mother, role model, strong independent woman. Relationships with child, parents, partner |
| ~24 employees (2005); earlier ~15 | Livelihoods. Hourly production and drivers plus Tracy Fields (finance and administration) |
| Five to ten exclusive small-farm producers in four states | Feed supplied by Nest Fresh; price contract by egg size; density rules |
| Jack Osborne (father) and Terry Osborne (brother) | Co-founding help. Ownership moved from 50/50 to Cyd two-thirds, Terry one-third. Terry stayed in caged production (Moark) and declined to run Nest Fresh |
| Cage-free coalition / Certified Humane partners | Industry standard and consumer education against the UEP Animal Care Certified seal |
| Buyers | King Soopers breakthrough (72 Denver stores, early 1993). Wild Oats lost in 2004 ($1.2M). Supermarkets and restaurants in 11 states |
| Large caged producers | Moark (family; Land O'Lakes acquisition in 2000), Rose Acre, Cal-Maine. Denver caged suitor who would not write the check. Cousin Paul at Moark/Land O'Lakes, approached twice |
| Consumers, mostly women in her telling | Health story first, welfare story second, at the start of the brand |
| Hens | The value she refuses to shade: no cages at supplier farms; integrity as the fundamental value |

### Facts worth having in discussion

**Industry.** By the 1970s, battery cages were the norm: birds eat less because they cannot move; the case says death rates for caged hens were estimated at nearly twice cage-free rates. About 300 million U.S. laying hens in battery cages. A buyer in the case: about 97% of eggs procured are from large caged complexes; about 3% are "designer" or specialty. Specialty (organic and cage-free) grew about seven-fold from 1997 to 2004 and was about 5% of the U.S. egg market by 2004. Cage-free prices in the case ran as high as 300% over conventional eggs. 2005 retail ranges in a footnote: large caged about $0.75–$1.25/dozen; cage-free about $2.09–$2.29; organic about $2.22–$2.60; omega-3 about $2.00–$2.40. EU: battery-cage ban mandated by 2011, with laws in place by 2003 and some countries earlier. "Cage-free" is not governed by one regulator. By 2004 about 80% of supermarket egg cartons carried the Animal Care Certified seal, which advocates in the case call misleading. By September 2005, seven large cage-free producers had Certified Humane certification.

**Cyd before Nest Fresh.** Southwest Missouri farm childhood. Family business became caged (Moark, started by grandfather and uncle). She is proud that she, unlike the rest of the family, has not worked in caged eggs. Master's in Christian education, Southern Baptist Theological Seminary; Sunday-school program work; divorce; healthcare marketing; Humana for about two and a half years; then Vail. Brother Terry raised cage-free at Christmas 1986. Jack started the cage-free effort in 1991 with a New Mexico supplier and a Colorado marketing push. Early volume about 50 cases a week at a price set to cover cost plus something. Terry would not leave Moark (750,000 pullets a year in Colorado at one point; three young children). Cyd took the company, moved to Denver, and found about $68,000 of debt she had not created. Both hired hands quit in week one. Two years of fear, 16–18 hour days. She knew the farm and did not know flock management; she hired that skill. King Soopers made Nest Fresh an early specialty egg in mainstream supermarkets rather than only health-food stores. She priced to cost plus a realistic profit, not to the Urner Barry commodity quote, and the buyer still took the eggs at over twice the caged price.

**2005 operating picture.** About 300–350 cases a day; 30 cartons per case; 12 eggs per carton; about 122,000 eggs a day. 75% of the Denver cage-free market; Denver the second-largest U.S. specialty market after San Francisco. 13 in-carton SKUs plus loose restaurant flats. Density at her producers: 144–225 square inches per bird. Caged industry average in the case: 48–62 square inches, cages stacked 6–8 high. Processing in Denver: clean, candle, grade, box. Leakers go to landfill. Hairline cracks ("breakers") go to a breaking plant for cake mixes, egg whites, frozen waffles. USDA date stamp; 30-day shelf life from processing. Colorado stores get direct ship; other states go through distribution centers. Brand work: chicken stories, press, speaking. Cartons carry her face. Profitable nearly every year since founding. Sales $5.6M in 2003, $5.3M in 2004 after losing Wild Oats. She reads the slip as the start of a trend. Opening of the case calls it her $5 million cage-free business after 14 years.

**Personal situation.** AnnaBelle's birth in 2000 triggered a strong wish to sell. She took a leave of about 18 months with an outside day-to-day manager. It did not work. Buyers have looked; timing has been wrong. She describes a renewed commitment to being the Egglady, and she wants growth only because competition may force it. "No margin, no mission" is her phrase for why the humane, health, and community work dies if the company dies. Community gifts and food-bank donations are part of how she defines business success, tied to the values.

### The three options (her framing)

1. **Stay the course.** Independent. Consumer education plus Certified Humane as an industry standard, with partners such as the Humane Society. She calls independence the best of all worlds and hopes she can afford it.
2. **Join a large caged producer.** She would need unusual operating independence to keep her integrity. A Denver caged owner wanted the brand and the market (the case says a deal would have meant about 90% of the Colorado egg market) and agreed she would speak only for the cage-free side. He did not write the check. Moark/Land O'Lakes via cousin Paul: she offered to run their cage-free piece in exchange for capital and cheap production; they declined twice. Her read: they can wait her out.
3. **Change the cost structure** so the current model stays profitable. Less developed than the other two. Ideas she names: new products that use breakers; a higher stocking density that is still cage-free ("a bit denser… not much denser"); European-style enriched cages, which she says would have to be sold as "barn eggs," not cage-free, and would still be more humane than factory cages. A caged barn in her comparison might hold four times as many hens.

She refuses profit that depends on shading the truth (organic claims without outdoor access, mixing caged eggs into a cage-free box, "cage-free" birds too damaged to leave an open cage). Integrity and credibility are the values she names as fundamental.

### Professor cues (Session 2) — discussion prompts, not the answer

- Which values matter, and how does she define success?
- Which operating features matter?
- Where is the evidence of creativity skills?
- What were Cyd's expertise strengths and gaps at the start?
- Where is intrinsic motivation? Where is extrinsic motivation (making a living)?
- Is the profit motive working with intrinsic motivation or against it?
- What is the current business situation and the personal situation?
- Which option fits her expertise, creativity skills, motivations, **and** situation?
- Slide hint the professor surfaces: option 3 as modifying the approach and the cost structure; using breakers; an enriched-cage possibility.
- Summary slide: egg farming takes creativity; values are a source of intrinsic motivation; setting core values aside is a mistake; pursuing them blindly is also a mistake; one value to keep in any option is her own well-being.

### Theory lenses (apply, do not dump)

| Lens | Where it bites |
|------|----------------|
| Amabile components | Marketing and brand expertise vs early flock-management gap. Creative-thinking: brand stories, coalition, pricing off the commodity quote. Intrinsic: hens as underdogs, pride, "all" of the work. Extrinsic: debt, fear for two years, payroll, a living |
| Six practices, applied to her as leader of 24 people | Challenge and match (she hired the flock skill). Freedom she gives herself vs the constraint a partner would impose. Resource threshold: margin squeeze. Work group: farmers as partners, not only competitors. Encouragement of the coalition. Organizational support she does not have: deep pockets |
| Innovator's DNA | Observing buyers and consumers; questioning the cage; experimenting with price, channel (mainstream grocery), and certification; networking the coalition; associating health and welfare stories. Delivery skill is the open question: who runs density, cost, and a possible partnership |
| Design thinking | Desirability (consumer and hen), feasibility (density, breakers, enriched cages), viability (margin vs Moark-scale cost). Risk of solving "how to stay cage-free forever" if the real problem is "how this company and this person stay healthy" |
| Intelligent failure / values | The 18-month exit attempt already produced information. A denser barn or a barn-egg line is a pilot only if she defines in advance what would falsify it |
| Exploration vs exploitation | Coalition education is exploration of the market's categories. Cost-structure change is exploitation of the existing brand. A caged partnership is a governance change, not a small experiment |

### Coaching questions

Ask one or two. Do not answer them for the student.

1. What is the decision in one sentence, and which of her own success criteria does that sentence leave out?
2. Which expertise is now the binding constraint: brand, flock and processing, cost, or coalition politics?
3. If intrinsic motivation is the hens and the truth-telling, which option spends that motivation down, and which one spends her health down?
4. What would count as a reversible experiment on density or breakers, and what result would make her stop?
5. The Denver deal and the Moark conversations already failed. What do those failures say about option 2's real availability, as distinct from its attractiveness?
6. Where is she at risk of functional fixedness ("cage-free" as the only humane category)?
7. Who is the delivery partner if she stays independent, and what happens to the 24 people in the downside of each option?

## Satera Team at Imatron Systems, Inc. (A) and (B)

**Cases:** HBS 803-141 and 803-142, Teresa M. Amabile and Elizabeth A. Schatzel. Company, division, and people are **fictional**. (A) copyright 2003/2007. (B) is the KAI exhibit, not a "what happened next" narrative.

### Situation in one paragraph

Early September 1999 (a cool Thursday). Gary Pinto, 45, team leader and senior mechanical engineer, has been called in by his boss Rick Levinger. The Satera team ("satellite camera") at Imatron Systems' Remote Imaging Division is designing a very small, light imaging system to document air and water pollution and coastal erosion from orbit. Prototype delivery to the U.S. government is contracted for 1 April 2000, with payment reductions if late. The project is a platform for later products and a piece of RID's 2000 and 2001 profit targets. Progress has stalled on the support structure that holds the imaging system and therefore gates the rest of the design. Levinger wants the Ira Lovas–David Bennett conflict handled now. Pinto has the afternoon to think, then a same-day meeting with Levinger.

### Company

ISI, founded 1983, private, Silver Spring, Maryland. About $300M revenue, 405 people, all in Silver Spring. Largest customer: U.S. government. Military and environmental imaging. RID is one of the newest and most profitable divisions. EPA collaborates with RID. In 1999 the revolutionary claim is sensitivity: very small environmental changes, graphically documented.

### Stakeholder map

| Who | Age | Role | Tenure / education | What they want in the conflict |
|-----|-----|------|--------------------|--------------------------------|
| Rick Levinger | — | Pinto's boss | — | The duel stopped; the date held |
| Gary Pinto | 45 | Team leader; senior mechanical; master's; patent; 15 years | Coordinates and guides the overall imaging-system design | A decision the whole team can build, and a way to make Ira and David collaborate after it |
| Ira Lovas | 44 | Senior mechanical; master's; patent; 12 years | Co-leads mechanical design | Room to decide and move. Experiences David as unable to let a decision stand |
| David Bennett | 39 | Senior mechanical; master's; patent; 9 years | Co-leads mechanical design | His ideas taken seriously. Has told Pinto he is not confident Ira knows what he is doing |
| Steve Rowling | 34 | Senior electrical; master's; patent; 10 years | Leads electrical design | The case quotes him on team dynamics; electrical needs must fit the structure |
| Jean Mahoney | 48 | Electrical; bachelor's, working on master's; 6 years | Electrical design | "Satera just never seemed to gel" |
| Katherine Baxter | 35 | Mechanical; bachelor's, working on master's; 3 years | Mechanical design | Says part of the team is dysfunctional and doubts the deadline. Finds Ira's prototype solid |
| Derek Delaney | 28 | Assistant mechanical; bachelor's; 1 year | Assists mechanical design | On the KAI chart; little airtime in (A) |
| U.S. government customer | — | Contract | Penalty for delay | A prototype that works, on the date |
| RID / ISI | — | P&L and platform | — | 2000–2001 targets; future products |

Six of seven hold or are earning a master's. Four members, including Pinto, Lovas, and Bennett, hold at least one patent (the fourth is Rowling, whose exhibit row is marked yes). All report to Pinto; junior engineers are primarily supervised by the senior engineers. Most are full time on Satera. Pinto's review line: they were selected because they like to work, not because they must be driven. Hours sometimes reach 19 a day, above typical ISI teams. Intrinsic motivation is high. Cohesion is not. Informal talk is rare. Pinto has caught information going to one senior engineer and not the other, and he sometimes briefs each person himself. Members rate their own and the team's creativity and work quality in ways the case says reflect weak work-group support (Exhibit 3 is a comparison chart; bar values are not printed in the text extract). Exhibit 2 compares Satera with other RID teams on mean conflict and mean progress; the extract shows the scale (0 to 6) and not the bar heights. Do not invent those scores.

### The technical conflict

Both engineers spent two weeks on separate mockups after a joint impasse. Both were vibration-table tested the day before the Levinger meeting. Both are feasible. Other members see that and are too intimidated to say so in the room.

| | David Bennett | Ira Lovas |
|--|---------------|-----------|
| Form | Small center, spoke-like extensions, a wheel. Passed around the room | Looks like a wrapped gift box; a honeycomb modeled on aircraft wings (lightweight, strong, holds components) |
| Test | After some time, spokes shift; the sensor shows damaging twist | Three minutes on the table; no significant problems |
| His argument | Light enough to give weight budget to other elements; the mission is revolutionary so the structure should be too. Deadline makes Ira's caution look like delay | Nature and aircraft practice. Critical thinking, several perspectives, integrated evidence. The government is paying for something that works. This is the wrong moment to reinvent the wheel |
| Pinto's private view | Attractive weight budget. David can be too unorthodox; Pinto has asked for a more methodical, less adamant style. Pinto admires him and has tolerated the aggression because of the ideas | Promising, possibly not light enough. Ira can be too conservative. Pinto has told him to present with more confidence and has sometimes pushed him toward newer technology. "Surefire" and timely; not the inventor of brand-new technology |
| Team after the meeting | — | Emails treat the honeycomb as the needed design despite the extra weight |

Pinto already believes the choice is Lovas's beehive over Bennett's wheel. He does not believe he needs more time to pick. He needs Lovas to accept Bennett's help making it lighter, and Bennett to believe the collaboration is worth his time. He is not sure collaboration is possible. He is sure the best design is the two of them hashing out details.

**Break-room scene (Pinto overhears).** Bennett: Ira blocks ideas before fair consideration. Ira: Bennett's process is undisciplined and wastes the team on far-fetched options. Bennett: low-risk will leave the company behind; he hopes Pinto still picks the wheel. Pinto hears sadness, not only anger. Ira's last word: Gary is smart and fair. Pinto does not share their confidence about his next move.

**Pinto's usual conflict method has already failed here.** On a previous team he used separate conversations, then a joint discussion. Praise plus honesty is his style. He takes input and uses it. He has tried social time. Lovas gets technical help from him on hard tests; Bennett gets a hearing. Hierarchy stays; the door stays open. None of that has moved this pair.

### What (B) reveals

HR ran a confidential company-wide survey (personality, work preference, cognitive style) for development and team building. Pinto wonders whether the cognitive-style measure can help him with Ira and David.

Instrument: Kirton Adaption-Innovation Inventory (KAI). Adaptors: precision, reliability, tried-and-true solutions. Innovators: ambiguity, tangential thinking, unprecedented solutions. Most people sit in the middle. Extremes are strong and hard to talk out of their approach. Kirton's point in the case: the styles are different **uses** of creative potential, not different amounts of it. A mix handles more contingencies than a team of clones, and the mix also clashes.

Population figures printed in (B): mean about 96, standard deviation about 17. Lower score = more adaptive. Higher = more innovative.

**Exhibit 1 is a bar chart without data labels.** Visual reads, about ±3 points, from the drawing:

| Person | Approx. KAI | Read against mean 96, SD 17 |
|--------|-------------|-----------------------------|
| Gary Pinto | ~108 | Moderately innovative |
| Ira Lovas | ~80 | Clear adaptor, near one SD below the mean |
| David Bennett | ~132 | Clear innovator, about two SD above the mean |
| Steve Rowling | ~91 | Mildly adaptive |
| Katherine Baxter | ~101 | Near the mean, slightly innovative |
| Jean Mahoney | ~117 | Innovative side |
| Derek Delaney | ~86 | Adaptive side |
| Satera team mean | ~102 | Slightly above the population mean |
| ISI company mean | ~101 | Near the team mean |

Use the pattern, not the exact integers, unless the student has the chart in front of them. The pattern that matters for class: Ira and David are at opposite extremes; Gary is on David's side of the mean, not in the middle; the rest of the team is spread between them; the company mean does not look like Ira.

### Professor questions (Session 4 slides)

Do not pre-write the answers.

- Strengths? Current overall health and performance?
- What problems? What is the central problem? What is the root cause?
- Cognitive styles: David, Ira, Gary?
- The dynamics slide (page 5): "Cognitive style difference" with an **unlabeled** arrow down to "Lower team effectiveness!" The mediator is not printed. Supplying one is the student's job (conflict type, communication, leader delay, intimidation, goal clarity).
- What makes an intra-team conflict like this worse? What makes it better?
- What should Pinto do now? What should he have done earlier?
- Role play: Gary tells them he has chosen Ira's support-structure design. He needs them to (1) make it significantly lighter and (2) finish the mechanical design of the prototype. Play the meeting.

Slides 9 and 10 are title-only ("What Should Pinto Do Now?" / "What Should Pinto Have Done Better Early on?"). No professor solution is in the extract.

### Theory lenses

| Lens | Diagnostic use |
|------|----------------|
| Thompson: cognitive vs affective | The vibration-table debate is cognitive / task conflict and is useful. The break-room exchange is personalized ("close-minded," "you don't know what you are doing") and is the slide's relationship-conflict risk |
| Creative abrasion | Pre-diagnose (the KAI is the late diagnosis). Warn the parachute builder before the leap. The clash is an approach difference with real technical content |
| Amabile work-group features | Diversity of style is present. Shared excitement, help through setbacks, and respect for the other's knowledge are the missing three. Homogeneous would have been faster and worse |
| Transformational leadership | Intellectual stimulation is David's oxygen and Ira's threat if it never converges. Individualized consideration is Pinto's strength and has not included an explicit style contract. Idealized influence: the government date and the science mission are available and under-used as a shared goal |
| Brainstorming liabilities | "When Ira and David brainstorm together" they evaluate immediately. That violates Osborn and matches evaluation apprehension plus status (two seniors, equal formal rank, unequal rhetorical force). Generate apart — they already did — then select with facts |
| Unique information | Electrical constraints and the weight budget are unique information. Intimidation kept it out of the room. Pinto's sequential one-to-one updates make shared information even stickier |
| Intelligent failure | Bennett experiences every shelved idea as a piece of himself removed. If Pinto picks the honeycomb without a designed role for David's lightening ideas, the lecture's "don't punish creative failure" test is failed |
| Time pressure | September 1999 to April 2000, with a penalty clause, is real urgency on a vital job (Amabile's condition for pressure that can still motivate). Nineteen-hour days are the "juggling" side. The structure decision is the ball that has not been made |
| Exploration vs exploitation | David's wheel is exploration that failed the test. Ira's honeycomb is exploitable. The remaining exploration is how to take weight out without losing the test result |

### Coaching questions

1. Is the central problem the support structure, the pair, Pinto's decision speed, or the team's inability to say what they see?
2. What is on the unlabeled arrow on slide 5? Name the mechanism in course language and point to a fact that shows it.
3. Given the KAI pattern, what is Pinto's own style risk in this meeting?
4. Which facts belong on the table before anyone's identity does (twist data, weight budget, electrical envelope, date, penalty)?
5. What would "make it lighter" mean as a joint task with a test, so David is not asked to applaud a loss and Ira is not asked to reopen a settled choice?
6. What should Pinto have done in the first month that the KAI, the separate mockups, and the silent room now make obvious?
7. In the role play, what sentence converts relationship conflict back into task conflict, and what sentence would do the opposite?
