> **Sits beside:** [`besremi-hk-pi-summary.md`](besremi-hk-pi-summary.md) (what we may say about the product) and [`pv-clinical-evidence.md`](pv-clinical-evidence.md) (which comparative numbers are cleared). This module does not expand either file.

## Why this module exists

Colleagues can recite a product dose and still be unable to explain why a myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) starts in one stem cell, why JAK2 V617F does not explain every patient, or why a normal blood count can already carry a clone that raises cardiovascular risk. That is the disease training. Product training comes after it.

## Compliance boundary (read aloud before the session)

- **Hong Kong on-label boundary stays the local PI.** Proactive BESREMi discussion is monotherapy in adults with polycythaemia vera **without symptomatic splenomegaly**. This lecture is not the label.
- **ET, myelofibrosis, CML, and the other MPNs** are classification and biology. They are not an invitation to discuss BESREMi efficacy, dosing, or access.
- **Interferon, hydroxyurea, and JAK inhibitors** appear as the lecturer’s map of treatment biology. Do not convert that map into a comparative claim, a ranking, or an IFN-to-IFN superiority statement. The HK PI says the potency of this product must not be compared with another pegylated or non-pegylated protein of the same class.
- **Hydroxyurea and secondary malignancy.** The lecturer argues against a mutagenic concern by pointing at long-term hydroxyurea use in sickle cell disease. That is his reasoning. It is not a cleared safety claim for or against BESREMi, and it is not a line for an HCP call.
- **CHIP and heart disease** are the lecture’s “big surprise.” Do not attach them to a product benefit.
- **Numbers below are the lecture’s citations**, paraphrased. If a physician asks for a figure that is not in the HK PI or [`pv-clinical-evidence.md`](pv-clinical-evidence.md), log it as reactive scientific exchange. Do not invent the missing figure.

## Objectives (a colleague can…)

1. Define an MPN as a chronic clonal marrow disorder arising in one multilineage hematopoietic stem cell, with signs driven by excess blood cells and their dysfunction.
2. Separate the diseases that already had a named driver around 2005 (CML, chronic neutrophilic leukemia, hypereosinophilic syndrome, systemic mastocytosis) from PV, ET, and myelofibrosis, which did not.
3. Explain JAK2 V617F as acquired, ligand-independent cytokine signaling, present in nearly all PV and about half of ET and primary myelofibrosis.
4. Explain why the lecturer treats the thrombopoietin receptor (c-MPL) as necessary for a JAK2 V617F MPN.
5. Describe treatment classes as mechanisms (cytoreduction, immune modulation, JAK inhibition) without ranking products.
6. Define CHIP and CCUS, and state who has a higher chance of progressing to a myeloid neoplasm.
7. Explain the cardiovascular finding: CHIP-mutant monocytes and macrophages drive IL-6–linked vascular inflammation, with risk that scales with clone size.
8. Refuse to promote ET, myelofibrosis, or “interferon is first-line for all MPNs” from this lecture.

## 1. What an MPN is

An MPN is a chronic malignant marrow disorder. One or more blood-cell lineages grow without normal regulation, and that growth can leave the marrow. The clone begins in a single multilineage hematopoietic stem cell. Symptoms and signs come from too many blood cells and from those cells working badly.

The lecture’s working list:

| Disease | Driver the lecture marks as known circa 2005 |
|---------|-----------------------------------------------|
| Chronic myeloid leukemia | t(9;22), BCR-ABL p210 |
| Chronic neutrophilic leukemia | t(9;22), BCR-ABL p230 |
| Hypereosinophilic syndrome | del(4q12), FIP1L1–PDGFR? |
| Systemic mast-cell disorder | KIT D816V (4q11–q12) |
| Polycythemia vera, essential thrombocythemia, idiopathic / primary myelofibrosis | Unknown in 2005 — marked “?” |

**Teach this contrast.** The “surprise” of the first half of the lecture is that PV, ET, and myelofibrosis joined the list of diseases with an acquired kinase driver. The surprise of the second half is that related mutations show up in people who are not sick.

**Facilitator:** Spend the time on PV, ET, and myelofibrosis biology. Name CML only to show what “a known driver” looked like before 2005. Do not open a CML treatment discussion.

## 2. How cytokine signaling set up the JAK2 discovery

JAKs are intracellular kinases. They do not float free: they have to be tethered to a dimeric transmembrane hematopoietic cytokine receptor and they cross-phosphorylate. Wilks described the first two in 1989; the family is JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2. The lecture’s publication count is the point, not a claim: 61 papers by 1994, 25,327 by 2025.

Before anyone found JAK2 V617F in patients, mouse genetics had already shown that too much cytokine signaling makes an MPN-like illness. The lecture lines up:

- Stronger erythropoietin-receptor signaling
- Loss of the brakes SHP-1 or SHIP-1
- Too much thrombopoietin receptor, a mutant thrombopoietin receptor, or transgenic thrombopoietin
- TEL–JAK2
- Loss of NF1, or oncogenic K-ras

**Clinical clue that predates the mutation:** bone-marrow cells from PV form erythroid colonies without added erythropoietin (Prchal, 1974). The same endogenous growth is seen in primary myelofibrosis and ET. That told the field the clone was signaling without its ligand.

**The mutation.** Baxter and colleagues (2005) found JAK2 V617F in patients with acquired MPNs. James and colleagues (2005) showed that putting JAK2 V617F into mouse marrow by retroviral transplant produces polycythemia. On the lecture’s diagram, the mutant kinase sits on the receptor and signals without the growth factor, downstream of the normal FOXO / p27 growth brake.

**Say:** JAK2 V617F is an acquired mutation in the stem-cell clone. It mimics a cytokine that never arrived.  
**Do not say:** “BESREMi inhibits JAK2.” It does not. JAK inhibitors are a different class. If someone blurs them, stop and separate the two mechanisms before you continue.

## 3. Why the thrombopoietin receptor is in a PV lecture

The lecture’s own experimental thread:

- Blocking the thrombopoietin receptor cuts the endogenous colonies that define these diseases (Li 1996; Kaushansky 1998).
- A Tie2-Cre model that turns on JAK2 V617F in hematopoietic stem cells and endothelial cells (Tie2-Cre driver from Kisanuki 2001; JAK2 model discussed via Tiedt 2008) is shown with higher colony counts (CFU-GM, CFU-GEMM, BFU-E, CFU-MK) and a blood-count panel for platelets and neutrophils versus wild type. Those phenotype slides are small (n = 3). Teach the design, not a number read off the figure.
- Crossing that JAK2 V617F model with an MPL-null mouse asks whether the receptor is required. The lecture’s conclusion, from the Sangkhae 2014 experiment: **c-MPL is essential for the JAK2 V617F MPN.**

**Pathobiology the lecturer wants remembered** (this is the hinge of the talk):

- In mice, mutations in several pieces of cytokine signaling (erythropoietin receptor, thrombopoietin receptor, JAK2, SHP-1) can cause an MPN.
- In people, JAK2 V617F underlies nearly all PV and about half of ET and primary myelofibrosis.
- CALR mutations are the second most common driver in ET and primary myelofibrosis.
- Patients without JAK2 or CALR often carry mutations in epigenetic modifiers.
- Most of these routes still depend on c-MPL, either directly or because the receptor gathers the kinases on the stem cell.
- Because of that, TPO or TPO-receptor antagonists are in hematology studies alongside JAK inhibitors. That is a research statement from the lecture, not a pipeline claim for this subsidiary.

## 4. Treatment, taught as mechanism

The lecturer’s sequence, paraphrased. Each bullet has a field translation.

**Not everyone is treated.** Watch-and-wait is sometimes reasonable, and the lecture says this is especially true in essential thrombocythemia. Many patients will still need treatment.

*Field translation:* ET treatment philosophy is context. It is not a BESREMi ET discussion.

**Phlebotomy can hold some PV, and then it often cannot.** Some PV is controlled by phlebotomy alone. Iron deficiency from repeated phlebotomy, sitting on top of the clone, tends to push the platelet count up and forces another therapy.

*Field translation:* Useful disease language for why hematocrit-only control is an incomplete plan. Do not upgrade it into “phlebotomy fails, therefore BESREMi.”

**Hydroxyurea** is anti-proliferative and is often first-line for PV and ET. Some clinicians worry it is mutagenic and causes second cancers. The lecturer’s counter-argument is long-term hydroxyurea in sickle cell disease without that signal. Separately, he notes that gastrointestinal intolerance and skin ulcers stop the drug for many patients.

*Field translation:* Intolerance is a clinical fact the lecture states. The mutagenicity paragraph is one expert’s rebuttal. Do not quote either as a promotional contrast.

**Interferons** are innate-immune cytokines that are usually anti-proliferative. The lecture describes them as treatment that recruits the immune system to restrain abnormal blood-cell growth. Pegylation makes them longer-acting. He says pegylated interferons are often used as first-line therapy in PV, ET, and myelofibrosis, and that dose is often limited by influenza-like adverse effects.

*Field translation:* “Often first-line” is the lecturer’s landscape sentence, covering three diseases and the interferon class. Hong Kong proactive use of BESREMi is adult PV without symptomatic splenomegaly. No class ranking. No comparison with peginterferon alfa-2a. Flu-like effects are a class counseling point only if Medical has cleared the wording; otherwise stay on the local PI adverse-reaction section.

**JAK inhibitors.** The lecture shows randomized trials under this heading, including one in 309 patients with primary myelofibrosis, and Harrison, *JCO* 2023, in 180 patients with PV intolerant of hydroxyurea. It does not put efficacy numbers on those slides in the text we distilled. Do not fill them in from memory.

He then widens the lens: JAKs also carry inflammatory signals such as IL-6, which is why JAK inhibitors are used in rheumatoid and psoriatic arthritis, atopic dermatitis, alopecia areata, and inflammatory bowel disease. The lecture lists nine agents as approved for MPNs **or** those other conditions: tofacitinib, upadacitinib, baricitinib, deucravacitinib, abrocitinib, ritlecitinib, deuruxolitinib, fedratinib, and ruxolitinib.

*Field translation:* Read the “or” carefully. This list mixes MPN drugs and immunology drugs. Do not tell a hematologist that each name is an MPN therapy or a BESREMi alternative. If an HCP asks about ruxolitinib in HU-intolerant PV, answer only from cleared reactive materials and name the study the HCP names. Do not borrow this lecture as the data slide.

## 5. What drives the patients who are not JAK2 V617F

The lecture points at Nangalia 2013, Klampfl 2013, and Michalak 2009 for the non-JAK2 drivers, and at Klampfl 2013 and Imai 2017 for additional mutations in ET.

Teaching version of the hinge in section 3:

- JAK2 V617F underlies nearly all PV, and about half of ET and primary myelofibrosis. The lecture does not give a finer split (exon 12, triple-negative) on these slides. Do not add one.
- CALR is next in those two diseases.
- The rest are largely epigenetic-modifier mutations.
- Across that mix, the lecturer’s thesis is that the signal still funnels through c-MPL.

**Do not** build an ET or myelofibrosis mutation pie as a product slide. If a physician asks, answer the biology and stop.

## 6. CHIP — the big surprise

### Neighboring clonal diseases, briefly

The lecture pauses on myelodysplastic syndromes and acute myeloid leukemia to make one point: they are also disorders of a hematopoietic stem-cell clone. Then it leaves them. Do not teach FAB subtypes from this deck.

### Definition

Once exome sequencing was cheap, healthy people who had been enrolled as controls for unrelated genome-wide studies were found to carry acquired mutations in the same genes that drive MPN, myelodysplasia, and AML, with no blood disease at all (the lecture cites Genovese 2014 in 12,380 unselected healthy Swedes).

**CHIP** (clonal hematopoiesis of indeterminate potential) entered the language in 2014:

- More than 10% of healthy people older than 70, with **normal blood counts**, have a detectable acquired mutation in an MPN-, MDS-, or AML-associated gene.
- The mutation sits in a stem cell and gives a proliferative or survival advantage, so a clone grows.
- A clone is called when it is **>2%** of blood cells. In the studies the lecture describes, the average clone at discovery in people in their seventies is about **20%**.
- By definition the blood counts are normal. The cells are not necessarily normal in function.
- **CCUS** (clonal cytopenia of undetermined significance) shares those mutations but adds one or more cytopenias, without the morphologic dysplasia of MDS.

Later in the lecture the prevalence is stated again, more fully: about **10–15% of people older than 70**, and **>30% by age 85**.

### Who goes on to a blood cancer

Jaiswal 2014, as the lecture shows it: among people with CHIP, the overall risk of a hematologic malignancy is about **5% at 8 years**, and the risk is higher when the mutant allele frequency is above 10%. A small 2018 ASH series (Rossi) is cited for a qualitative point: in that set, only people whose clone evolved went on to a hematologic malignancy (5 of 22 showed clonal evolution).

Weeks and Ebert, *Blood* 2023, as the lecture uses it:

- Risk depends on the mutation and on the blood counts, not on “CHIP” as one bucket.
- A single **DNMT3A** mutation has the lowest risk of progression.
- **Splicing-factor** mutations, and **TP53, IDH1, IDH2, and RUNX1**, have the highest risk of evolving to a myeloid neoplasm.
- Clone size (variant allele frequency) and the number of mutations are also prognostic.

Jan and colleagues (2012) supply the mechanism picture: residual “normal” stem cells in patients who already have AML often carry the same mutations seen in CHIP. Those mutations look like the soil for later AML drivers.

The lecture’s closing line on this risk is a **~10-fold** increase in hematologic malignancy, varying with the mutation. Teach the 10-fold figure and the ~5% at 8 years as two statements from two slides (relative risk in the conclusion; an absolute risk on the Jaiswal slide). Do not multiply them into a new number.

### Where the mutations come from

The lecture’s account:

- Likely genotoxic stress, not a mystery germline disease.
- In a Chinese series of more than 10,000 people treated for non-hematologic cancers, CHIP was present in **9.1% under age 40** and **23.1% over age 80**. A US series of more than 9,000 people with solid tumors looked similar. Non-genotoxic targeted therapies were not associated with CHIP.
- Cytotoxic chemotherapy is associated with more **TP53** and **PPM1D** mutations, particularly platinum and radiation.
- Deep sequencing before and after chemotherapy suggests a second, important sequence: in therapy-related myeloid cancers, CHIP is often **already there**. Chemotherapy may select and transform a pre-existing clone rather than write the mutation from scratch.
- Cigarette smoking has a moderate association and tracks with epigenetic-modifier mutations such as **ASXL1**.
- Common germline variants also tilt the odds. An 8-base-pair intronic deletion in **TERT** was associated with a **1.4-fold** increase in CHIP driver mutations, which the lecturer reads as a telomere-maintenance clue.

### Why a hematologist is looking at coronary arteries

This is the surprise the title promises.

CHIP is an important risk factor for coronary disease, heart failure, and stroke, and for death from all causes. The lecture’s mechanistic story:

- Atherosclerosis is inflammatory (the lecture traces the idea from Virchow to Russell Ross, 1979).
- Activated endothelium recruits monocytes. Those monocyte-derived macrophages make inflammatory cytokines.
- CHIP mutations change that behavior. Zhang 2015: TET2 normally restrains IL-6 transcription while inflammation is resolving. Lose TET2 and IL-6 and other mediators stay high; TET2-deficient mice do worse in endotoxin shock and in colitis.
- Bick and colleagues, *Circulation* 2020: a common IL-6-receptor variant that **weakens** IL-6 signaling lowers cardiovascular events in people who have CHIP, compared with CHIP carriers who have the usual receptor. The same variant does nothing to cardiovascular risk in people **without** CHIP. Risk also rises with clone size, which the lecturer treats as dose-response evidence.
- Supporting clinical slides he names: Jaiswal 2017, Fuster 2017, and Gumuser, *JACC* 2023 (665 people who already have atherosclerotic disease and also have CHIP).

The Jaiswal 2017 comparison slide, as the lecture prints the relative risks (point estimate and interval):

| Condition | Relative risk |
|-----------|----------------|
| Diabetes mellitus | 1.47 (1.02–2.08) |
| Smoking | 1.71 (1.39–2.10) |
| Dyslipidemia | 1.74 (1.36–2.23) |
| Hypertension | 1.92 (1.41–2.59) |
| Job strain | 1.34 (1.18–1.52) |
| Inflammation | 1.3 (1.1–1.6) |
| Depression | 2.5 (1.53–4.43) |
| CHIP | 2.0–4.0 |

**Teach the comparison as the lecture’s slide, not as a local epidemiology claim.** CHIP’s cardiovascular relative risk on this slide sits in the same range as the classical risk factors, and above several of them. Absolute risk for a given patient is not on the slide.

**Slide the text extract does not spell out.** One section header is “Six more surprises,” with no bullets in the text distillation. Open the source PDF for that slide. Do not invent six findings.

**Lecture conclusions to close on:**

- CHIP in ~10–15% after age 70, and >30% by 85.
- Unexpected risk factor for cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.
- Mechanism: vascular inflammation from clonally derived monocytes and macrophages.
- About a 10-fold higher risk of a hematologic malignancy, depending on the mutation.
- A prevention question for both blood cancers and cardiovascular disease in older adults — posed, not answered with a protocol.

## 7. Case — do not skip this

The lecture ends on a hypothetical 72-year-old, otherwise well, with controlled mild hypertension and mild hypercholesterolemia and no other heart-disease risk factors, who asks whether he should be tested for clonal hematopoiesis.

The slides do not answer. A good facilitation does not invent a society guideline.

Push the room to use only what the lecture supplied:

- At 72 he is inside the age band where CHIP is already common (about 10–15% over 70), so a positive test would not be rare.
- A positive test is not a diagnosis of PV, ET, or myelofibrosis. Counts are normal by definition.
- The malignancy question depends on which gene, how large the clone is, and how many mutations — DNMT3A alone is not TP53.
- The cardiovascular question is real on this lecture, and it scales with clone size and with IL-6 signaling, but the lecture does not give a treatment to start because a test came back positive.
- Therefore “should he be tested?” is a question about what he would do with the answer. The lecture’s point is that the answer can change how you think about heart disease and about future myeloid risk. It is not a product moment.

## Knowledge check (facilitator key)

Use these verbally. A pass is 6 of 8, including questions 4 and 8, which are the compliance items.

1. **Where does an MPN clone start?** One multilineage hematopoietic stem cell. Signs come from excess blood cells and their dysfunction.
2. **Which classical MPN lacked a named driver in 2005?** PV, ET, and myelofibrosis. CML already had BCR-ABL.
3. **What fraction of disease does the lecture assign to JAK2 V617F?** Nearly all PV; about half of ET and primary myelofibrosis. CALR is the next driver in ET and myelofibrosis.
4. **A colleague says “pegylated interferon is first-line for ET and myelofibrosis, so we can say that.” What do you do?** Stop. That sentence is the lecturer’s class-level landscape. It is not the HK indication and it is not a proactive claim.
5. **What did the MPL-null cross show?** c-MPL is required for the JAK2 V617F MPN in that model (Sangkhae 2014, as the lecture titles it). Do not quote a blood count from the n = 3 graphs.
6. **Define CHIP in one sentence.** An acquired, expanded hematopoietic clone (over 2% of blood cells) in a person with normal blood counts and no hematologic diagnosis; common after age 70.
7. **Why does the lecture connect CHIP to coronary disease?** Clonally derived macrophages keep IL-6 signaling high (TET2 is the example). An IL-6-receptor variant that weakens that signal reduces cardiovascular events only in people who have CHIP. Risk rises with clone size.
8. **Name two things this lecture must not become.** Any two of: a BESREMi ET/MF claim; an IFN-versus-IFN comparison; a hydroxyurea-causes-leukemia claim; a JAK-inhibitor efficacy slide; a CHIP screening recommendation; a statement that BESREMi inhibits JAK2.
